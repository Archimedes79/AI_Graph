The Map with a Gap

Mirela Costa drew maps for the provincial survey office, and she was good enough at it that her maps were trusted without being checked. That was the problem. In the spring she was sent to chart the valley of the Orsa, and she charted all of it but one square kilometre, because on the day she reached that square it rained so hard that she turned back, and afterwards the season ended and the money ran out.

A careless cartographer would have filled the square with guesswork: a few contour lines continued from either side, a plausible stream. Nobody would have known. Mirela left it white, and wrote across it in small capitals: NOT SURVEYED.

Her supervisor, a tidy man named Dorn, objected. A map with a hole in it looked unfinished. Clients would complain. "Draw what is probably there," he said. "You have walked every hill around it. You know what is probably there."

"I know what is probably there," Mirela agreed. "That is why I will not draw it. If I draw it, the map says I know. The white square is the only honest part of the sheet — it is the one place where the map tells you exactly how much to trust it."

Dorn published the map with the gap, mostly because reprinting was expensive. Two years later a road crew planning a route through the valley saw the white square, distrusted it as they were meant to, and sent a man to look. He found a sinkhole forty metres across, hidden in the beech wood, directly on the line the road would have taken.

The survey office framed the map afterwards and hung it in the entrance hall. Visitors assumed the white square was damage and sometimes offered, kindly, to have it restored. Mirela, who by then ran the office, always gave the same answer: that the map was complete, and that the blank was the most carefully drawn thing on it.
