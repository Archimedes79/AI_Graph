The Second Key

When old Tobias Reuter died he left his clock shop to his apprentice, Jonas, together with a ring of forty keys and a note that said: thirty-nine of these open something. Jonas spent the first winter finding out what. Cabinets, the cellar, the case of the long regulator clock, a cash box containing eleven marks and a button. By March he had matched thirty-nine keys to thirty-nine locks, and one small brass key was left over.

He tried it everywhere. He tried it in locks it plainly would not fit. He asked the neighbours, the locksmith, and Tobias's sister in Lindau, who said her brother had never thrown anything away in his life and had never explained anything either.

For a year the key hung on a nail above the workbench, and it changed how Jonas worked. Because there might be a fortieth lock, he looked at the shop more carefully than he would otherwise have done. He lifted floorboards and found nothing but the wiring, which was dangerous, and which he replaced. He took down the panelling and found dry rot, and cured it. He opened the back of every clock in the storeroom looking for a hidden compartment, and in doing so repaired nineteen clocks that Tobias had given up on, and sold them.

It was the locksmith who finally said it, turning the key over in the light. "This was never cut for a lock. Look — the teeth are just filed to look right. It opens nothing. Somebody made it to be left over."

Jonas walked back to the shop, which was by now sound, dry, safely wired and solvent, and he understood the note at last. Thirty-nine of these open something. The fortieth had opened everything else.

He hung the key back on its nail. Many years later he took on an apprentice of his own, a quick, impatient girl named Hanne, and when he retired he left her the shop, a ring of forty-one keys, and a note.
