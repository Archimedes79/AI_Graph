The Lighthouse Keeper's Ledger

For thirty-one years Ansel Brandt kept the light at Skerry Point, and for thirty-one years he wrote the same thing in the ledger every evening: the hour the lamp was lit, the direction of the wind, and the number of ships that passed. The Harbour Board had asked for the first two. The third was his own idea, and nobody had ever read it.

When the Board sent a young engineer named Ilse Varga to install the automatic lamp, Ansel showed her the machinery with the politeness of a man handing over his own house keys. She worked for three days. On the fourth morning the new lamp turned by itself, exactly on time, and needed no one.

"You can go home now," she said, and then, because he did not move, "Where is home?"

He did not have a good answer. Instead he brought down the ledgers, eleven of them, and set them on the table between the tea cups. Ilse turned the pages out of courtesy at first, and then more slowly. In the margins, beside the ship counts, Ansel had drawn small marks: a circle for a vessel that signalled back, a cross for one that did not. There were a great many crosses in the early books and almost none in the late ones.

"They learned you were here," she said.

"They learned somebody was," Ansel said. "A lamp tells a ship where the rocks are. It does not tell a ship that anyone would notice if it hit them."

Ilse wrote her report that night. She recommended that the automatic lamp be kept, since it was reliable and cheap, and that the keeper be kept as well, since he was neither. The Board, to its own surprise, agreed. Ansel went on lighting nothing and counting everything, and on clear evenings the ships still signalled back to a tower that no longer needed a man — two short flashes, which among sailors on that coast had come to mean: seen, and thank you.
